    Model card¶

    Following the structure of Mitchell et al., Model Cards for Model Reporting (FAT* 2019). It covers the models amesml trains with its defaults; the numbers come from scripts/benchmark.py and the held-out split described below.

    Model details¶

    • Developer: Muhammad Ahsan, Software Engineering, COMSATS University Lahore.
    • Version: amesml 0.1.1 (November 2026).
    • Type: supervised tabular models built with scikit-learn.
      • Regression predicts the sale price in US dollars.
      • Classification predicts whether a house sells at or above a threshold price - by default the median sale price of the training rows ($165,000 on the reference split).
    • Families: ordinary least squares / logistic regression (the original notebook's models), ridge and lasso (L2- and L1-penalised), and histogram gradient boosting.
    • Inputs: up to 79 property attributes from the Ames dataset (size, quality and condition ratings, age, location, amenities, sale type).
    • Licence: MIT.
    • Contact: GitHub issues.

    Intended use¶

    • Primary use: teaching, learning and demonstrating a reproducible, production-style machine-learning workflow: packaging, testing, serving and documenting a model end to end.
    • Primary users: students, instructors and engineers studying ML engineering practice.
    • Out of scope: pricing, buying, selling, lending, insuring, taxing or appraising real property. The models describe one Iowa city between 2006 and 2010; they do not know today's market, other regions, or anything not in the 79 columns. Do not use them for decisions that affect people.

    Factors¶

    The data contains no information about buyers, sellers or occupants. Relevant factors are properties of the houses: price band, age, neighbourhood, size and quality. Neighbourhood is a feature, and because neighbourhood composition can correlate with protected characteristics, location-driven predictions can encode historic patterns of segregation and lending (see Ethical considerations).

    Metrics¶

    • Regression: mean absolute error (dollars; easy to interpret), root mean squared error (penalises large misses), R² and mean absolute percentage error (comparable across price levels).
    • Classification: accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and ROC-AUC. The classes are balanced by construction (the threshold is a median), so accuracy is meaningful; ROC-AUC measures ranking independent of the threshold.
    • Uncertainty: a single 292-house test split is noisy, so models are also compared with 5-fold cross-validation (mean ± standard deviation).

    Training and evaluation data¶

    • Dataset: the Kaggle House Prices: Advanced Regression Techniques training file - 1,460 residential sales in Ames, Iowa, 2006-2010, with 81 columns (an Id, 79 candidate features and SalePrice). It is a subset of the 2,930-sale dataset compiled by De Cock (2011) from the Ames City Assessor's Office.
    • Preprocessing: NA means "absent" (no pool, no basement) and becomes an explicit Missing category; numeric gaps take the training median, except veneer area and garage year where a gap means "none" and becomes 0. Category codes (MSSubClass, MoSold) are one-hot encoded. All statistics are learned from the training rows only.
    • Split: 80% training (1,168 houses), 20% held out (292), shuffled with seed 42 - the notebook's split.

    Ethical considerations¶

    • Uneven accuracy falls on cheaper homes. If these models were used to value property, owners of lower-priced and older homes would see the least accurate estimates relative to their home's value - the same groups for whom an error matters most. This is the main reason the intended use excludes real decisions.
    • Location as a proxy. Neighbourhood strongly drives predictions. In housing, location-based valuation has historically carried discriminatory lending and appraisal patterns; a model trained on past sales reproduces past prices, including any bias in them.
    • No personal data. The dataset describes properties and sales, not people.

    Caveats and recommendations¶

    • The data is from 2006-2010, one city, and includes the start of the 2008 housing crash. Prices since then are not represented.
    • Four sales have more than 4,000 sq ft of living area. Two are priced like ordinary houses ($160,000 and $184,750) and are true outliers; De Cock recommends removing houses over 4,000 sq ft for teaching use. The models keep them, as the notebook did.
    • A handful of categories occur only in the test split (for example the Membran roof material); unseen categories are reported, not silently guessed.
    • Prefer the cross-validated scores when comparing models; the single split's differences are often within noise.
    • Retrain and re-evaluate before any use outside this dataset.

    How to reproduce¶

    pip install "amesml @ git+https://github.com/Ahsan-muhd-444/house-price-prediction@v0.1.1"
amesml train                                  # notebook linear regression
amesml train --task classification            # notebook logistic regression
amesml train --model gbm
python scripts/benchmark.py                   # every number on this page

    Each saved model's metadata.json records the configuration, library versions and a SHA-256 fingerprint of its training data.
